A1 — Before the buttons: water, roots and air

Start with the pot: roots need access to water and oxygen. A sensor describes only a small part of the root zone.


The question

Before you use the dashboard, identify the conditions inside the pot.

What do the roots need?


Inside the root zone

The pot contains solid material, water, and air. The solid material around the roots is the substrate. Coco and stone wool are examples of substrates.

There are spaces between the solid parts. These spaces contain water or air. Roots need water and oxygen.

More water does not always improve conditions for the roots. The substrate must hold water and let excess water drain. Air must also return to some of the spaces.


Watch water arrive and drain

Look at the spare container. Water enters the substrate. Some water stays in the substrate. Other water drains into the tray.

The amount of water then decreases. The plant uses some water. Evaporation and drainage also remove water.

A decrease in water content does not show only the water that the plant used.


Pause before adding water

This pot stays very wet. The leaves look unhealthy. Does this mean that you must add more water?

Choose one answer. Add more water immediately. Or do a check to find the cause first.

Do a check first.

Different problems can cause similar symptoms. The graph gives useful information. It does not identify every problem in the roots.


What steering and sensing can do

Crop steering means control of conditions to influence crop growth. These lessons explain irrigation. Irrigation supplies water to the crop.

The important irrigation settings control the time and quantity of each water delivery. The interval between deliveries is also important. Light, temperature, and humidity change the demand for water.

Later lessons explain vegetative steering and generative steering. These are directions of crop management. They do not guarantee a specific yield. Maximum drying is not the objective. The roots must continue to function correctly.

A sensor measures conditions at a specific location. One sensor does not measure every pot. A satisfactory reading does not prove that every dripper is clear.

Use sensor readings with equipment checks and crop observations.

The demo lets you learn the software without operation of a real pump. You do not need all the equipment to use the demo. For a new irrigation system, start with one zone.

Point to the solid material in the drawing. Point to the water. Point to the air. Give one example of information that the sensor cannot supply.

The next lesson explains volumetric water content, or VWC.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Compare your reasoning: Pore spaces contain water or air. Roots need oxygen as well as water. A local probe cannot establish every container’s condition.

A2 — VWC: what does 60% actually mean?

Use equal volumes to understand volumetric water content, then separate VWC from plant-available water.


Recall before the reveal

The previous lesson showed three parts inside the pot.

Name these parts before the answer appears.


Six out of ten

The three parts are solid material, water, and air.

VWC means volumetric water content. It is the volume of water divided by the total volume of the sample. The result is usually a percentage.

The drawing divides a sample into ten equal volumes. Water occupies six of these volumes. Six divided by ten is zero point six. Multiply this result by one hundred to get sixty percent.

This example has sixty percent VWC. The blocks explain the volume ratio. In a real substrate, water occupies spaces between solid parts. It does not form the separate blocks in this drawing.


Put litres beside the fraction

This example uses a representative sample with a total volume of five liters. Its VWC is sixty percent. Five multiplied by zero point six is three. Thus, the sample contains three liters of water.

This calculation explains the amount of water already in the sample. It is not an instruction to add three liters to a five-liter pot.


The probe sees a region

Sixty percent of the total volume is water. Is all the remaining forty percent air?

No. The remaining volume includes solid material and air. VWC alone does not give the complete air volume.

Sixty percent VWC does not mean that sixty percent of the water available to the plant remains. Some water stays more tightly attached to the substrate. Different substrates hold water differently. VWC alone does not show the time until the plant needs water.

Look at the probe location. The probe measures a local region. Its installation, contact, calibration, and location affect the reading.

A probe near one dripper might not represent a dry pot at the end of a row. Later lessons explain how to select locations that represent the zone.


Teach it back

The sensor now shows forty percent VWC. Does this reading always mean that the substrate is too dry?

No. The reading alone is not sufficient. You also need information about the substrate, validated reference values, crop, and conditions. One VWC target is not correct for every system.

Do not select a target yet. Complete this sentence. Sixty percent VWC means what?

Approximately sixty percent of the measured sample volume is water. This is one correct answer.

The reading does not give the exact water volume available to the plant. One sensor does not prove that all pots have the same conditions.

Use these limits when you assess advice about VWC.

The next lesson explains why a full pot does not mean one hundred percent VWC.

Compare your reasoning: 40% VWC. The remainder includes solids and air; it is not automatically 60% air or 60% usable water.

A3 — When is a pot full? Capacity versus saturation

Separate saturation, a documented drained container condition, and the program’s configured field-capacity reference.


What does full mean?

A grower asks for a full pot. What does full mean?

VWC is water volume divided by total sample volume. The total sample volume also includes solid material. A correctly irrigated pot does not need a reading of one hundred percent VWC.


Saturation is a physical state

Saturation is one physical condition. At saturation, water fills the pore spaces. Pore spaces are the spaces between solid parts of the substrate. The solid material remains present.

Continuous saturation around the roots is not the objective.


Observe drainage

Water drains from the spare pot into the tray. After a specified drainage procedure, some water remains in the substrate. Some spaces contain air again.

The retained water quantity depends on the substrate and container conditions. Container height is one of these conditions. Container capacity describes the retained water under specified container and drainage conditions.

Growers sometimes call their reference value field capacity. The program also uses this term. Field capacity originally describes a condition in field soil. A pot or substrate block has different physical conditions.

Identify the drainage method that applies to the reference value.

One drainage time is not correct for all pots. Use a documented method suitable for the substrate and system. Record the container type. Record the substrate type. Record the initial condition. Record the drainage interval.

Record the probe installation. Do the procedure again. If the results change, find the cause before you use the reference value.

Container weight and visible drainage give additional information during the procedure. A suitable sensor gives another measurement. These measurements do not correct poor probe contact or a location that does not represent the zone.


Physical state or entered setting?

The dashboard contains a reference setting. A person entered this value into the program. The displayed value does not prove that the measurement procedure was correct.

Keep a measured physical condition separate from a software setting.


An example is not your target

The example reference value is sixty percent. Must your substrate use the same reference value?

No. Sixty percent is only the example value. Your reference value needs measurement evidence for your substrate and container. The measurement method must also be suitable.

The highest sensor reading is not automatically the correct reference value. A temporary peak does not prove correct calibration. Poor contact or an incorrect reading does not establish a reference value.

There are three cards in this exercise. The first card is saturation. The second card is drained container capacity. The third card is the reference setting in the program.

Identify the cards that describe physical conditions. Identify the card that describes a software setting.

Saturation and drained container capacity describe physical conditions. The configured reference is a software setting. A useful setting represents a suitable measured condition. A value without measurement evidence is not sufficient.

The next lesson explains dryback. Dryback is a decrease in substrate water content between irrigation events. A decrease of fifteen percentage points is different from fifteen percent relative dryback.

Compare your reasoning: The cards describe saturation, a drained container condition and a configured reference. Only a suitably documented measurement supports the actual container condition.

A4 — Dryback without the confusing maths

Describe a water-content drop in percentage points and as a percentage of its starting value.


One drop, two descriptions

Recall the ten equal volumes from lesson A2. VWC describes how much of the measured sample volume is water. It does not say how much usable water remains. Now compare two readings from the same probe. A fall in water content over an interval is called dryback. We can describe that fall in two ways. First name the starting and ending readings, then state which calculation you used.


Subtract to get percentage points

Our teaching sample starts at sixty percent VWC and ends at forty-five percent. Subtract forty-five from sixty. The drop is fifteen percentage points. A percentage point is the difference between two percentage readings. We have not yet calculated a relative percentage. Keep both timestamps with the readings. This example explains arithmetic. It does not establish a suitable dryback for a crop, a container, or a medium.


Divide to get relative dryback

For relative dryback, ask what fraction of the starting reading was lost. Divide the fifteen-point drop by the starting value of sixty. The result is one quarter. Multiply by one hundred to express it as a percentage. That gives twenty-five percent relative dryback. The denominator means the number you divide by. Use the starting reading. Using the ending reading answers a different question. A zero starting value cannot be used in this division.


Pause and complete the steps

Pause and complete this partly filled example. The start is eighty percent and the end is sixty percent. Eighty minus sixty gives a drop of how many points? Put that same drop over eighty, then multiply by one hundred. Resume when you have both labels. The answers are twenty percentage points and twenty-five percent relative dryback. Notice that the relative result matches the previous example, although the point drop differs.


Try new numbers without the steps

Now work independently. A fresh record starts at fifty percent VWC and ends at forty percent. Write both descriptions before you continue. The point drop is ten. Ten divided by fifty, times one hundred, gives twenty percent relative dryback. Saying only ten percent dryback would hide your convention. When you hand over a record, include the two VWC readings, the interval, the calculation, and the result with its label.


Read a program field carefully

The planner guide defines morning dryback relative to a peak. Suppose its peak is sixty percent and the example field says ten percent relative dryback. Ten percent of sixty is six. Subtract six from sixty to get a fifty-four percent VWC reference. Do not subtract ten points unless a field explicitly uses points. Read the help for the actual field. A configured reference describes a rule; it does not prove the crop reached it.


Check meaning before making a claim

Finish by recalling A3. A configured reference is different from a measured physical condition. The same distinction applies here. A calculated dryback describes two observations only when their units, probe identity, and timestamps are trustworthy. Water can leave the measured region through drainage and evaporation as well as plant use. This calculation does not separate those paths. Tomorrow, explain the difference between points and relative percentage aloud, using numbers you choose yourself.

A5 — EC: what it measures, and which EC you are looking at

Identify feed, bulk, pore-water and runoff EC before comparing readings.


A number needs a measurement label

A meter shows a number beside EC. EC means electrical conductivity. It describes how readily an electrical current passes through the measured material. Dissolved charged particles, called ions, influence conductivity. The number does not identify each nutrient. Before interpreting it, find the measurement type, unit, whether the meter adjusts for temperature, and the sample method. Recall A2: a familiar percentage can hide a wrong meaning. An EC number also needs its full label.


Follow the sample locations

Follow four labelled places. Feed EC comes from the solution supplied to the plants. Bulk EC describes the combined medium around a probe. Pore-water EC describes the water within the spaces in that medium. Runoff or extract EC describes a collected sample made by a stated procedure. Collection methods matter. A drainage cup and a laboratory extract are not interchangeable simply because both contain water from the root region.


Worked example: label before comparing

Here is a worked record. A tank sample measures two point zero millisiemens per centimetre. Label that value feed EC. A TEROS twelve probe reports zero point eight in the same unit from the medium. Label its direct conductivity output bulk EC. The unequal numbers alone do not prove an error. We measured different things. Copy the sample location and method into each record before you consider a comparison.


A conversion needs evidence

A display may calculate pore-water EC from other measurements. That calculation is a model, with assumptions and a valid operating range. Check the documented method and its required inputs. A label change is not a conversion. Do not compare a feed reference with a bulk reading as if they were the same measurement. Ask the installer what the displayed reading represents. Keep an unknown type marked unknown until its origin is established.


Pause and finish the labels

Complete this partly labelled diagram. The supplied solution is already labelled feed EC. What label belongs to a cup collected using a documented drainage procedure? What label belongs to water within the medium, measured or estimated by an identified suitable method? Pause first. The answers are runoff EC and pore-water EC. Keep the collection procedure with the runoff result. These labels describe evidence. They do not prescribe a target to copy.


Try a new record independently

Now try a new record independently. The supply sample reads one point seven millisiemens per centimetre. A drainage cup reads two point four. Label both readings and state what else you need before interpreting the difference. Check collection timing, method, meter checks, and whether the meter adjusts for temperature. This pair cannot identify an excessive individual nutrient. It also cannot establish the pore-water EC throughout every pot. Different media and methods need locally justified interpretation.


Use EC with the rest of the record

Recall dryback from A4. A correct calculation still needs trustworthy readings and a stated interval. Use that same discipline with EC. Write the type beside every value, compare like methods, and retain the crop and substrate context. This course gives no universal substrate EC target. A surprising number starts an investigation. It does not justify adding fertiliser, flushing a crop, or changing a protection without the responsible operator and supporting evidence.

A6 — Can you trust that reading?

Check identity, freshness, units, placement and calibration before interpreting a trace.


A smooth line can be old

A graph looks steady. Does that mean the root region is steady? First check when the last valid observation arrived. Freshness means the information is recent enough for the decision. A browser can keep showing an old value. A flat line can also be a real steady reading. Its shape alone cannot decide between those cases. Recall A5: first establish what a number measures. Now add when and where it was measured.


Worked example: calculate the age

Work through a synthetic record. The clock says fourteen hundred. Probe A last supplied a valid observation at ten hundred. Its data are four hours old. Probe B in another representative location updated at thirteen fifty-nine. The difference raises a sensing question before a crop-response question. Record both identities and ages. Check availability, communication and power through the approved procedure. Do not turn an old reading into a watering instruction.


Match identity and units

Next match the room, zone and probe to the physical label. Similar names are not proof. Then read the unit. A VWC value of zero point four five cubic metres per cubic metre equals forty-five percent. Treating zero point four five as zero point four five percent creates a hundredfold scale error. For EC, retain the measurement type from A5. Read the original reading and displayed reading together when tracing a mismatch.


Check what the probe represents

Calibration is the method that relates the sensor signal to the reported quantity. Confirm that the documented calibration suits the medium. Check installation records and physical placement with the responsible person. Poor contact or an air gap can distort a moisture reading. A probe beside one emitter may not represent another part of the container. Agreement between probes helps, but two probes can share the same error. Do not average away an unexplained disagreement.


Pause and finish the check

Complete this partly finished check. It is fifteen thirty. The last valid observation was fifteen twenty-five. How old is it? Identity and units already match. Name another check before accepting the whole zone as represented. Pause now. The age is five minutes. Next checks can include suitable placement, calibration records, and comparison with independent evidence. Five minutes describes age only. It is not a universal guarantee that any control decision is safe.


Try a changed scenario independently

Work independently on changed numbers. At sixteen twenty, one display still shows forty-eight percent from twelve twenty. Another representative probe reports forty-three percent at sixteen nineteen. State the age of each result. Then write three checks in order. Start with identity and last valid observations. Check availability and the communication path. Follow with a suitable physical or independent check. Do not conclude that every pot is dry from either single number.


Restore evidence before changing targets

Recall A3: the configured capacity reference is not proof of physical capacity. Likewise, a plausible display is not proof of valid sensing. Independent evidence may include a documented reference measurement and a competent physical inspection appropriate to the installation. If sensing is unreliable, follow the local safe fallback procedure and involve the responsible operator. Do not lower a protection to force watering. Your exercise ends with a reasoned check record, without changing live equipment.

Compare your reasoning: A is three hours old; B is one minute old. Current zone uniformity is unknown. Verify identity/timestamps, availability/communications, then suitable physical and calibration evidence.

B1 — Read water and EC on the same graph

Read both axes, find day and night, and keep recorded evidence separate from a planning reference.


Start with time

This graph shows water content and EC over time. We will examine one part at a time.

Time is on the bottom axis. Later times are farther to the right. The shaded area shows the night period in this example. Night shading does not mean that the sensor stopped measurement.


Add water content

The first line shows VWC. Read this line against the percentage scale on the left.

This point shows sixty percent VWC at the indicated time. The solid line connects the example observations.

In a live system, do a check of the reading age. Identify gaps in the readings.


Add EC and a second scale

EC means electrical conductivity. The EC line uses the scale on the right. The unit is millisiemens per centimeter.

Identify the EC measurement assigned to the graph.

Feed EC, bulk substrate EC, and pore-water EC describe different measurements. These names are not interchangeable.

The VWC line and EC line cross on the screen. Does this mean that the measured values are equal?

No. VWC and EC have different units and scales. Each line shows how its measurement changes with time.

Do not subtract a VWC value from an EC value.


Events, observations and references

The markers show irrigation events. Water content can increase after water reaches the probe. Water content then decreases between events.

EC also changes. The EC line does not need to be the opposite shape of the VWC line. Water delivery, mixing, drainage, temperature, measurement method, and crop conditions affect the readings. One line does not prove the cause of a change.

The graph also shows a planning reference. The program draws this reference from the selected settings. A measured line shows observations. A planning reference shows the behavior illustrated by the software. These lines need different labels, even when their positions are the same.


Continue into night; move a threshold

The planning reference continues through the night to the next morning. An unexplained break makes the reference difficult to read. A continuous line does not prove a correct prediction of the crop response.

The overnight EC reference connects the last daytime reference to the next morning reference. This connection is not a separately scheduled P3 EC target. It does not calculate or prove the behavior of salts overnight.

Now watch this demo edit. We increase the P3 emergency VWC threshold in the local draft. The draft threshold changes from thirty-five percent to thirty-nine percent. The saved threshold remains visible for comparison. The measured example readings remain the same.


Explain what changed

Which item changed? The previous water content? The local draft of a threshold? Or a guaranteed irrigation event?

The local draft of the threshold changed. Review and application of the settings are separate actions. The controller uses its conditions and protection rules to decide if irrigation occurs. The emergency threshold is not a normal overnight target.

B2 — P0 to P3: build the day one phase at a time

Explain the program’s four phases and separate a projected timetable from actual decisions.


A phase is part of a control sequence

A phase is one part of the program sequence, with its own purpose and rules. This program names four phases: P zero, morning dryback; P one, ramp-up; P two, maintenance; and P three, overnight. Those names belong to this program. Recall B1: time belongs on the horizontal axis, and actual readings differ from reference lines. A phase label helps explain the rule in use. It does not prove that water flowed.


P0: begin with morning dryback

At configured lights-on, the program begins a new grow-day in P zero. Morning dryback allows water content to fall before the ramp begins. Recall A4: a relative dryback uses the peak as its denominator. The program also has timing rules, including a maximum wait. Do not assume the first shot must occur at a fixed clock time. Read the reported phase and reason, and confirm the lighting schedule against the actual installation.


P1: build water content with shots

A shot is one watering event. P one uses shots to build water content toward the configured peak target. The program checks conditions between events and applies its limits. A target is a reference used by a rule, not a promise of the next probe value. The real response depends on delivery, drainage and the sampled region. Do not infer the number of completed shots merely from the steps drawn on a planning curve.


P2: maintain with conditional events

P two is maintenance. A re-water threshold is a reference used when deciding whether another shot is needed. Conditions and protections still matter. A planning chart may draw several evenly spaced events using an assumed drying rate. The actual controller responds to its rules and readings. A slower decline can mean fewer events than the chart shows. Check recent sensor data and the next-action explanation before you interpret a missing shot as a fault.


Worked example: explain the sequence

Work through a fictional lighting day from six in the morning to six in the evening. Start with P zero at lights-on. Explain the morning dryback, then P one ramp-up, then P two maintenance. P three covers the overnight period; the rules can end daytime watering before lights-off. We cannot calculate exact transition times from the lighting hours alone. Record the displayed reason and actual observations instead of treating the illustration as a timetable.


P3: keep the emergency floor separate

P three covers overnight dryback. Its emergency floor is a separate protection reference, used by the overnight rescue rule when its conditions permit. It is not an ordinary target that the operator should try to reach every night. Recall A6: if a reading is unreliable, investigate and follow the local fallback procedure. A low or old number is not permission to bypass a hold. The physical system still needs the approved handover and protections.


Complete, then explain a new day

Pause and complete the two missing phase purposes: P zero is what, and P two is what? They are morning dryback and maintenance. Now independently explain a different lighting day, from eight in the morning to eight in the evening. Put the four phase cards in order. State which exact shot times remain unknown. Explain why moving the emergency floor would change a rule reference without rewriting the measured water-content trace.

B3 — Vegetative and generative: a bias, not a magic switch

Understand steering direction and how the planner blends operator-defined endpoints.


Name a direction before a setting

Vegetative growth includes roots, leaves and shoots. Generative growth includes reproductive structures, such as flowers and fruit. A steering bias is a management direction intended to influence growth. It is not a guarantee of a plant response. A flowering crop can still receive a vegetative bias for a particular purpose. Recall B2: phase names describe parts of the watering day. Steering direction and watering phase describe different things, so keep both labels clear.


The crop sees more than one setting

Irrigation changes root-region conditions. Climate and light also affect the response. More water availability can support a vegetative direction, while greater dryback can contribute to a generative direction in suitable conditions. These are broad concepts, not instructions to increase stress. The same settings can behave differently with another medium, plant variety, crop stage or climate. Use local measurements and the responsible grower’s judgement. This lesson supplies no recipe or substrate EC target.


What the planner slider actually does

An endpoint is one set of configured values at the end of the slider. A profile holds both endpoints for reuse. The planner has a vegetative endpoint at zero and a generative endpoint at one hundred. Between them, it blends each supported setting. The operator supplies those endpoint values. The slider does not discover safe limits from the container size. Existing installations may begin with equal endpoints. In that case, moving the slider changes no effective value until endpoints differ.


Worked example: halfway between endpoints

Use fictional P zero wait endpoints to understand the arithmetic. One endpoint is thirty minutes and the other is fifty. Their difference is twenty minutes. A slider at fifty uses half that difference: ten minutes. Add ten to thirty to obtain forty minutes. The program rounds to the step supported by the actual setting. This example illustrates blending only. It does not establish a suitable morning wait for any crop or installation.


Pause and complete the blend

Complete the partly filled example before reading the result. Keep the endpoints at thirty and fifty minutes. Move the slider to twenty-five. What fraction of the difference does that use? Pause now. The fraction is one quarter. One quarter of twenty is five, so the arithmetic result is thirty-five minutes before any required rounding. Now ask what happens if both endpoints are thirty. Every slider position produces thirty because their difference is zero.


Try new endpoints independently

Now work independently with new endpoints: twenty and sixty minutes. The slider is seventy-five. Calculate the result before you continue. Three quarters of the forty-minute difference is thirty. Add that to twenty to obtain fifty minutes. State two limits of this answer. It is arithmetic, not a recommendation. It does not predict the actual crop response. Remember that other supported endpoint settings also blend; inspect the whole profile rather than one number.


Review scope and evidence

Recall A5: an EC value needs a measurement type before comparison. Apply that discipline when reviewing endpoint EC fields. Also check units, the intended zone, and every schedule block using a shared profile. A profile edit can affect those references when the plan is applied through its workflow. Previewing a slider change is not applying a plan. Save, arm and activation are separate ideas taught later. Practise with fictional data and record the limits of your conclusion.

B4 — How much water did each plant get?

Calculate runtime-based delivery per plant and per zone, then state what those estimates cannot prove.


Keep the unit and scope together

A dripper, also called an emitter, delivers water to a container. Its flow rate describes volume per time. Litres per hour is shortened to L slash h. First find how many drippers serve each plant. Then identify whether the flow value is nominal, meaning rated, or measured by collecting water during a timed catch test. Keep per plant and per zone separate. Recall A2: VWC measures water in a sampled region. It is not a delivery meter.


Worked example: delivery per plant

Our fictional plant has two emitters, each at four litres per hour. Multiply four by two to get eight litres per hour per plant. The completed shot lasts forty-five seconds. There are three thousand six hundred seconds in an hour. Multiply eight by forty-five, then divide by three thousand six hundred. The result is zero point one litres, or one hundred millilitres per plant. This is an estimate if the input flow is assumed.


Scale the estimate to the zone

The zone contains twenty plants. Multiply zero point one litres per plant by twenty plants. The zone estimate is two litres for one completed shot. Ten identical completed shots would estimate twenty litres for the zone, or one litre per plant. That statement assumes the same effective runtime and flow each time. Effective runtime means the time actually used after limits. Count completed events, not the symbols on a planning chart. Conditional events mean a whole-day total cannot be guaranteed from the plan alone.


A shot fraction is not a VWC rise

Each example plant has five litres of substrate. Divide the delivered zero point one litres by five, then multiply by one hundred. The shot equals two percent of substrate volume. That does not guarantee a two-percentage-point rise in the probe’s VWC. Water can drain or spread unevenly, and the probe samples only part of the container. Recall A4: a measured water-content drop also does not tell us plant uptake by itself.


Pause and finish the calculation

Complete a partly filled example. Each plant has one emitter at three litres per hour. A completed shot lasts sixty seconds. Multiply three by one by sixty, then divide by three thousand six hundred. Pause and fill the gaps. The result is zero point zero five litres, or fifty millilitres per plant. With twelve plants, multiply by twelve to obtain zero point six litres per zone. State whether the flow was measured or nominal.


Use the effective runtime

The requested time may differ from the effective runtime after a duration limit. If the request is one hundred twenty seconds but the effective runtime is sixty, estimate delivery using sixty. The guide separates those values. A runtime record still cannot prove equal flow at every emitter. Pressure, blocked outlets and other faults can change delivery. Supervised commissioning uses actual catch measurements and verifies that water starts at the intended outlets and then stops.


Independent task: new numbers and a clear claim

Now solve a new problem independently. Each plant has two emitters at three litres per hour. The completed shot lasts ninety seconds, and the zone has fifteen plants. The answers are zero point one five litres per plant and two point two five litres per zone. Delivery means water supplied. Runoff means water leaving by drainage. Uptake means water entering the plant. A delivery estimate alone measures neither runoff nor uptake. Write one sentence that states that limit.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

A fictional zone has 18 plants and two 2 L/h emitters per plant. Effective runtime is 75 seconds. Calculate litres and millilitres per plant, then zone litres. Explain why the result is not uptake.

Compare your reasoning: About 0.0833 L = 83.3 mL per plant; 1.5 L per zone. These are flow/runtime delivery estimates, assuming the stated flow, and do not measure uptake or distribution.

C1 — What you need: the smallest useful system

Plan equipment by function: host, measurement, water path, control and independent physical protection.


Five functions, not a shopping spree

Start with one zone.

The system needs five functions. A computer runs the program. A sensor measures water content. The irrigation equipment supplies water. Control equipment operates the water delivery. Protection equipment and verification procedures help prevent incorrect operation.


Host and measurement paths

The host is the computer that runs the software. One option is a supported Raspberry Pi installation. Another option is a supported dedicated Intel NUC or other x86-64 computer. You need only one host option.

Use the correct power supply. Use suitable storage. Connect the host to a reliable network.

The two host lessons explain alternative installations. You do not need both installations.

Home Assistant connects the devices. It gives their readings and controls names called entities. Crop Steering uses these entities. The Crop Steering integration and the separate Controller app are both necessary parts of the installation. A dashboard alone does not control irrigation.

The next function is measurement. Use a compatible moisture probe at a location that represents the zone. Do a check of the exact sensor model. Do a check of the interface. Do a check of the units. Make sure that the sensor is suitable for the substrate.

Some probes need a separate bridge. Matching wire colors do not prove compatibility. One working probe does not prove equal water delivery to all pots.

Root-zone EC supplies additional information. Identify whether the sensor measures bulk substrate EC or pore-water EC. Tank feed EC is a different measurement.

Do not rename tank feed EC as root-zone EC.

Air temperature and humidity give additional information about water demand.


Draw the actual water path

The next function is water delivery. A suitable pressurized supply can supply a zone valve without an additional pump. A reservoir system needs a pump if the supply cannot provide the necessary water pressure.

If the system needs a pump, include the pump in the design. Include its necessary protection equipment.

Filtration, pressure regulation, drainage, and backflow or siphon protection must suit the installed equipment. The design must also meet applicable local requirements.


Safe control and proof

The next function is safe control. The control hardware must have suitable ratings and a correct installation. A pump has a starting load as well as an operating load. A large power rating on a household smart plug does not prove suitability.

Use an appropriately qualified installer for electrical work. Do not do mains electrical work as part of this course.

Only one controller must control each physical pump or valve. An old irrigation system must not also operate that output.

Resolve shared control before you enable the new controller.

The final function is verification and protection. The system needs an accessible physical shutdown and suitable independent protection. Test water must have a safe collection location. A software OFF command is not an emergency isolator.

Use collection cups and a stopwatch to measure water delivery. Make sure that water physically stops after the stop command.

A changed dashboard icon does not prove that the water stopped.


Essentials, conditional, later

Tank level, feed EC, pH, and tank temperature give useful operating information. Additional probes and a verified flow meter supply more information later. Automated dosing, thermal cameras, and artificial intelligence are not necessary for the first working zone.


Your own layout

Draw the water path. Label the water supply. Label the pump, if installed. Label the zone valve. Label the drippers. Label the drainage path.

Draw the probe separately. Draw the controller separately. Identify the physical shutdown. Identify the controller for each output.

Circle each item that you cannot identify.

Use the equipment sheet to do a check of exact models before purchase. Select one host lesson. The options are the Raspberry Pi lesson and the x86-64 lesson.

After host installation, both options use the same setup procedure. Install one zone first. Assign its sensors and outputs. Verify its operation before you add more zones.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

On paper, trace your proposed water path. Mark the host, measurement path, controlled outlet and physical shutdown. Identify which parts need an installer’s confirmation. Do not operate live outputs.

Compare your reasoning: The diagram identifies every function and the actual installation’s physical stop. Product compatibility and physical commissioning require local evidence.

Enabling permits eligible shots, including safety watering. Follow current documented controls and readback. Keep safe collection and physical shutdown available. Observe the intended outlet, other zones, actual cessation, event record and next action. If water continues unexpectedly, use the agreed physical shutdown, disable Watering and resolve the fault. Finish in an explicitly agreed state and repeat commissioning for each new output.

D1 — Find your room, zone and next action

Read identity, freshness and control scope before deciding what to do.


Start with identity

Open the isolated demo. This lesson uses sample rooms, not your installation. Overview starts with All zones, grouped by room. A zone number belongs to its room; Zone 2 in one room is not Zone 2 everywhere. Select the intended room, then open its zone card in Selected room. Say the room and zone aloud before you inspect a setting. This small habit prevents a large scope mistake.


Read age before value

Look at the age of the controller report in the header. Then inspect the probe reading, its unit and its latest report. These describe different sources. A fresh controller report does not prove that every probe is fresh. Unknown means the program cannot confirm a state. Do not translate an unknown pump into OFF, or missing water content into zero. Recall A6: a smooth trace can still contain old information.


Read a decision in context

The zone page brings together its phase, latest irrigation and Why / next action. Read the reason before interpreting an absence of shots. The zone might be waiting for a condition, or it might have a problem. Inspect pump and tank information where available, including Equipment, Tank and feed. An event record and an output state answer different questions. Neither alone proves water reached every emitter or that a valve physically stopped flow.


Worked example: choose the right scope

Here is a fictional screen: Training East is selected, Zone 2 is in P2, and the last shot is at nine twelve. Why / next action says it is waiting for moisture. First check that reports are recent, then read the reason. Header Watering and Stop all apply to Training East. Stop all can also stop a pump shared with another room, so read its caution. It is never proof of physical isolation.


Complete the missing check

Complete a partly filled record. Training West, Zone 1, is selected. The pump state says unknown. Write: I can confirm the selected identity, but I cannot confirm the pump state. Next check the mapped source and its report age using the safe local procedure. Do not press controls to discover which equipment they operate. Before live operation, an installer must already have proved the mapping and taught you the physical shutdown procedure.


Try another zone without help

Choose a different demo room and zone without following the example. Record its phase, last irrigation, controller age and next action. State which information is unavailable instead of guessing. In Settings and help, Settings, Display, choose Default view for this browser. That preference changes where this browser opens; it does not change irrigation. A direct bookmark still opens its requested page. Return to Overview and check the room identity again.


Recall before you leave

Close your notes and answer three questions. Which room would the header controls affect now? What does an unknown output state tell you? What evidence proves water stopped? The answers are the selected room, an unconfirmed state, and an appropriate physical check. The demo teaches navigation but cannot prove plumbing or hardware. Tomorrow repeat this check on a different sample zone. Live operation still requires your completed local handover and commissioning record.

D2 — Change one target and see the curve move

Practise draft, review, apply and readback without confusing a reference with a measurement.


Find who owns the targets

Open Irrigation, Current day, in the isolated demo and select a zone. Read Target source before touching a field. Current day is the manual target editor when no plan owns these targets. Calendar supplies dated targets through a plan. These are views of the same control workflow, not independent live modes. If a plan owns the targets, use a suitable manual demo zone for this exercise. Do not disarm a real plan for practice.


Separate three kinds of line

Read the chart legend. A saved reference represents the configuration available to the view. A draft reference represents your local edit. The actual trace represents observations recorded from probes. Moving a draft line changes the proposed reference immediately. It does not move the measured crop or rewrite its history. The planning curve illustrates rules and assumptions. It is not a validated forecast of water content, root uptake or salt response.


Know this field before editing

Select the help beside the P3 emergency floor. This is an overnight safety threshold, not an ordinary target to chase all night. Changing it can change when a safety rule becomes eligible; it does not command a guaranteed shot. Conditions and protections still matter. Record the existing value and allowed step. All exercise numbers are fictional. Finishing the interface practice does not establish an appropriate threshold for a live crop.


Worked example: one local step

Suppose the demo floor is thirty percent VWC and the allowed step is one point. Make the local draft thirty one. The draft floor moves while the saved reference stays available. Open Review changes and read room, zone, field, unit and before and after values. Only applying this reviewed request sends targets to Home Assistant in a real connection. Our isolated demo updates sample state. Neither situation removes the need for readback.


Complete the sequence

Complete this sequence: draft, review, blank, readback. The missing action is apply. After applying in the demo, inspect the returned value and any visible error. A request is not proof of acceptance. If acceptance is unclear, investigate that result before sending another change. Restore the original floor through the same review and readback steps. You have practised a reversible software workflow, including its return path, without selecting a crop recommendation.


Try a different example

Use another manual demo zone with its own saved value. Predict which line will move before you make one allowed step. Check room and zone in the review without using the earlier numbers. Apply, read back, then restore and read back again. Explain what would change if the selected field were room-wide. A zone-specific value can override a shared room value, so a room edit does not prove every zone uses that number.


Recall the measurement boundary

Recall A4: one VWC percentage point is not one percent relative dryback. Read the unit and definition of each field rather than copying numbers across fields. Recall D1: selected scope matters on every review. Before interpreting the result, check freshness as in A6. Explain this lesson without the screen: local draft, reviewed application and readback are separate. Changing a reference does not rewrite observations or prove water was delivered.

D3 — Plan days and weeks without losing control

Separate schedule drafting, stored plans, arming and scheduled handover.


One room, one target owner

Open Irrigation, Calendar, in the isolated demo. Calendar defines dated targets for the room’s zones. Current day shows effective targets when a plan owns them; it is not a second independent active mode. Read plan status and ownership before editing. An armed or active plan must be disarmed before changes. Disarming is a scheduled handover: wait for draft status. Choose an already editable demo plan rather than changing live operation for this exercise.


Endpoints define the balance

Expand Calendar and profiles, then open Endpoint profiles. Its vegetative and generative columns contain operator-defined settings. The balance slider blends those settings; it does not invent a safe recipe from pot size. Equal endpoints give equal values at every slider position. A profile can serve several blocks and zones. Before editing, find all its references. Duplicate the profile when a zone needs an independent version. The word profile does not limit a change to the visible cell.


Worked example: a dated block

Use fictional P2 shot-size endpoints of two and four percent of substrate volume. A fifty percent balance gives three percent, before rounding to the supported step. That calculation describes a setting, not a measured VWC rise. Give the demo zone its own start date and assign the profile to days one through seven. Check adjacent blocks for gaps or overlaps. A day number belongs to that zone’s start date, not to every zone automatically.


Complete save, arm and activation

Complete the workflow: local draft, Review and save, stored draft, blank, next local lights-on. The missing action is arm. Saving stores a plan; it does not start it. Arming requests activation at the next local lights-on using configured room hours. It does not enable Watering or start a pump. Examine validation and controller status. If a handover is incomplete, report that uncertainty rather than saying the plan is active because you pressed a button.


Disarm and hold have limits

Disarm does not instantly restore manual targets or isolate water. Active targets remain until the next lights-on handover. Review what manual values will return, then wait for draft status before editing. A plan hold also does not prove no water: safety watering may still occur under controller rules. For uncontrolled flow, use physical shutdown. Recall D1: header Watering and Stop all address the selected room, with possible shared-pump consequences.


Try a different block and profile

Try another demo zone and days eight through fourteen. Identify its start date and list the other blocks that use its selected profile. Predict the effect of changing one endpoint before making an independent copy. Preview and review your demo draft. Explain the next steps without arming a real plan. Recipes under Calendar and profiles saves reusable copies in this browser, site and room. A library save changes no active Home Assistant target; loading creates local draft work.


Recall the separate boundaries

Without notes, explain three boundaries: saving versus arming, arming versus Watering, and disarming versus physical isolation. Recall B3: a steering balance is a bias between configured endpoints, not a guaranteed crop outcome. Tell another learner which zones a shared profile change could affect and when a plan change could become effective. Repeat tomorrow with different start dates. Accurate control timing matters more than memorising the fictional endpoint numbers in this demonstration.

D4 — Compare reality with the plan

Write observations that respect dates, measurement types and missing evidence.


Choose the question first

Open History in the isolated demo. Choose the question before the chart. Timeline helps investigate events and changes. Water use shows delivery records. Compare runs helps inspect available traces across runs, and Grows manages run records and summaries. These views describe different evidence. Begin with room, zone and date range. A convincing chart from the wrong zone or an incomplete interval does not answer your question, even when its line looks familiar.


Match the comparison basis

Before comparing, match elapsed grow age. A complete older run and the first week of a current run cover different opportunities for water use. Check dates, time context, zone identity and mapped sensors. Read the units and EC type; bulk EC and pore-water EC are not interchangeable. Look for coverage notes and missing periods. Data the Home Assistant recorder did not retain cannot be recovered merely by drawing a longer axis.


Worked example: a fair observation

Suppose two fictional runs each have complete day-ten records for the same zone definition. Run A reports twelve litres estimated, and Run B reports fifteen. The supported observation is that reported estimated delivery was three litres higher in B for that matched day. It does not prove the plants absorbed three litres more. It also does not prove a recipe caused the difference. Climate, plant count, flow assumptions and other changes need inspection.


Keep reference time separate

A reference line is not automatically historical evidence of targets in force. A saved run can preserve a timestamped configuration. If you create an older run record today, it captures today’s reference unless an existing saved reference is available. It does not reconstruct last month’s targets. Saving that record also starts no irrigation. Label the actual trace, reference source and snapshot date separately. Ask what records would establish the target used at a past moment.


Complete the cautious statement

Complete this statement: runtime multiplied by configured flow gives estimated delivery, not measured blank. The missing word is uptake. A per-plant figure is an average based on count; it does not prove equal emitters. Recall B4: water can drain and distribution can vary. A suitable verified water measurement can support a measured total, but still does not measure plant uptake by itself. Preserve source labels when exporting numbers or writing a handover.


Try an incomplete comparison

Investigate a different example independently. One display covers day six only; another covers days one through six, and one EC series is missing. Decide whether totals are comparable before calculating a difference. Write one observation supported by the available period and one conclusion the data cannot support. In the demo, choose another zone or date range and repeat. Do not replace missing EC with zero or infer that a missing line means no salts.


Recall before making a claim

Close the chart. Explain the difference between an actual reading, a stored reference and a current illustrative reference. Explain why creating an old run today cannot rebuild lost history. Recall A5: name the EC sample type before interpreting its value. Your final comparison should contain scope, matched interval, units, source and coverage limits. If evidence supports only an observation, keep it as an observation. A useful honest limit guides the next measurement.

D5 — Troubleshoot before you change a recipe

Protect the installation, verify evidence and resolve the fault before target changes.


Separate danger from interpretation

A problem on a chart is not always a crop problem. First decide whether water is uncontrolled or equipment is unsafe. If water continues unexpectedly, use the installation’s physical shutdown procedure immediately, following your local handover. Do not wait for a chart refresh. For uncertain readings without uncontrolled flow, preserve evidence and follow the installation’s safe fallback procedure. Lowering a floor, bypassing a hold or forcing outputs is not a valid diagnostic shortcut.


Worked example: stale moisture

A fictional zone is holding and its moisture timestamp is four hours old. A friend suggests lowering the threshold. Reject that shortcut because the controller still lacks trustworthy moisture evidence. Confirm room and zone, inspect the mapped entity and report age, and check communications and probe condition through the safe local procedure. Compare appropriate independent observations. Record the hold reason. Restore reliable sensing and verify recovery before evaluating a target change.


Tank disagreement stays unresolved

The tank display says eighty percent, but an installed low-level indication conflicts. Do not average them or trust the more reassuring value. Treat supply readiness as unresolved. Follow the safe local procedure to check the physical level, mapped sources, calibration and timestamps. Keep installed protections in use and isolate as required. Do not start a pump to test whether the tank is empty. A screen percentage cannot resolve a contradictory physical indication.


Saved does not mean accepted

Check configuration as well as sensors. Similar entity names do not prove which valve or probe belongs to a zone. Setup can be saved while the controller has not accepted the revision. Inspect acknowledgement, heartbeat and setup problems. Keep affected watering disabled until the accepted configuration is established under the local procedure. Correct outlet mapping must be proved through commissioning. A recent heartbeat proves communication, not that the intended outlet waters or water stops.


Complete the emergency response

Complete the emergency response: the app says OFF but visible flow continues. First, physical shutdown. Once the installation is safe, investigate a sticking valve, siphon, output mapping, feedback and conflicting control owners with competent help. Header Stop all is a selected-room software action; readback alone cannot prove cessation. Disarming a plan waits for a lights-on handover, and a plan hold may allow safety watering. Neither replaces the physical procedure for uncontrolled water.


Try a different fault report

Try a different case without the worked steps. The selected zone shows no rise after a shot, while a neighbouring zone rises. Write what is observed and what remains unproved. Check identity, timestamps and mapping evidence; also consider probe placement and delivery. Follow the safe local procedure and seek competent help for equipment tests. Do not force each valve in turn. Report room, zone, event time, expected result and actual result without exposing credentials.


Recall the order

Recall D2: a target edit changes a rule’s reference, not a failed sensor or pipe. Recall A6: stale and missing data are not zero. State the troubleshooting order: make safe, preserve evidence, investigate the source, then verify the repair and agreed final state. Physical recommissioning needs competent local supervision. Passing this lesson does not prove hardware safety. Finish by explaining where your knowledge of local shutdown still needs a handover.

D6 — A five-minute daily check and a useful handover

Make one defensible decision and leave the next operator a clear record.


Start a short reliable record

A daily check should help the next person understand what happened. Open the isolated demo and begin a five-part record: identity and freshness, state, water, feed and context, then decision. Five minutes is a useful routine, not a time limit for an unresolved fault. If evidence is stale or water is uncontrolled, follow D5 instead of rushing through. State explicitly when a source is unavailable. Do not fill the record with guessed zeros.


Read state and water together

Record room, zone, date and time, then the ages of the controller and relevant probes. Read phase, Why / next action and last irrigation together. Note actual output status only where confirmed. For water, include the zone total and per-plant average with source labels. Recall B4: runtime-derived litres are estimates, and delivery is not uptake. An unusual total needs context, not an immediate recipe change based on that total alone.


Worked example: choose no change

Here is a fictional record. Training North, Zone 3, has recent reports, an expected maintenance phase and a clear waiting reason. Estimated delivery is within its recent observed range, and tank indications agree. The justified decision can be no change. Record why and when you will check again. A daily check is not a requirement to move a slider. If you adjust a target, record exact before and after, reason and expected observation.


Complete a useful change record

Complete this change record: before and after, scope, reason, expected observation, review, apply, blank, and follow-up. The missing step is readback. Explain what result would support the decision and what would make you investigate. Change one thing when justified. If several changes are necessary, record them together so a later comparison does not pretend to isolate a single cause. One different day does not establish that the target change caused it.


Use AI as bounded assistance

Crop AI can help organise a question or explain a proposal. Its advice is not verified crop evidence. Check source readings, timestamps, room, zone, units and assumptions before relying on an explanation. A suggested action does not prove Home Assistant accepted anything; inspect readback and physical evidence where needed. Keep credentials out of prompts and screenshots. Ordinary operation does not require advanced AI configuration. Ask for a source-based explanation when the proposed action is unclear.


Try a different day and handover

Use another demo zone with one unavailable source and complete the record independently. Decide whether the evidence supports no change, investigation or one justified demo adjustment. Explain the choice. Prepare a handover naming the responsible operator, software versions, backup location and recovery procedure. A visible backup entry alone does not prove recovery. Before an update, preserve a recoverable backup and follow the installation’s update and rollback guide. Leave unresolved checks and the agreed final state visible.


Teach back and revisit

Teach a second person to find the correct room and zone, check freshness and identify control scope. Demonstrate the change workflow only in the isolated demo. For a real installation, a competent local supervisor must teach physical shutdown and verify handover; an online quiz cannot establish physical competence. Tomorrow recall draft, apply and arm without notes. About a week later repeat a stale-data scenario with different values. Keep records brief enough to use and specific enough to trust.

A1 — Before the buttons: water, roots and air

Start with the pot: roots need access to water and oxygen. A sensor describes only a small part of the root zone.


The question

Before you use the dashboard, identify the conditions inside the pot.

What do the roots need?


Inside the root zone

Picture a handful of gravel. The pieces are solid, but there are gaps between them. A pot also contains solid material and spaces. The growing material around the roots is called substrate; coco and stone wool are examples.

Those spaces can contain water or air. Roots take up water, and they also need oxygen. Filling every space with water leaves less room for air.

So pouring in more water is not always helpful. The substrate needs to hold water, let excess drain away, and allow air back into some spaces.


Watch water arrive and drain

Look at the spare container. Water enters the substrate. Some water stays in the substrate. Other water drains into the tray.

The amount of water then decreases. The plant uses some water. Evaporation and drainage also remove water.

A decrease in water content does not show only the water that the plant used.


Pause before adding water

This pot stays very wet. The leaves look unhealthy. Does this mean that you must add more water?

Choose one answer. Add more water immediately. Or do a check to find the cause first.

Do a check first.

Different problems can cause similar symptoms. The graph gives useful information. It does not identify every problem in the roots.


What steering and sensing can do

Think of driving a car: you make adjustments to keep it on course. Crop steering means adjusting growing conditions to influence growth. Here we focus on irrigation, which means supplying water to the crop.

With irrigation, you choose when water arrives, how much arrives, and how long to wait before the next delivery. Light, temperature and humidity also change how much water the crop needs.

Later we will name two steering directions: vegetative and generative. They describe management intended to influence leafy growth or reproductive development. Neither guarantees yield. Drying the pot as much as possible is not the goal; roots must keep working.

A thermometer in one room cannot tell you the temperature in every room. A moisture sensor has the same local limit: one reading does not describe every pot or prove every dripper is clear.

Use sensor readings with equipment checks and crop observations.

Practise in the demo first; it does not operate a real pump. For a new installation, start with one zone: a group of plants whose water delivery is controlled together.

Point to the solid material in the drawing. Point to the water. Point to the air. Give one example of information that the sensor cannot supply.

The next lesson explains volumetric water content, or VWC.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Draw an unplanted pot. Label solids, water and air. Explain why one local moisture reading cannot prove that every plant has the same conditions.

Compare your reasoning: Pore spaces contain water or air. Roots need oxygen as well as water. A local probe cannot establish every container’s condition.

A2 — VWC: what does 60% actually mean?

Use equal volumes to understand volumetric water content, then separate VWC from plant-available water.


Recall before the reveal

The previous lesson showed three parts inside the pot.

Name these parts before the answer appears.


Six out of ten

The three parts are solid material, water, and air.

Think of ten equal boxes making up the whole sample. How many boxes worth of water are inside it? That is the idea behind volumetric water content, or VWC: water volume divided by total sample volume, shown as a percentage.

In the drawing, water takes up six of the ten equal volumes. Six divided by ten is zero point six. Multiply by one hundred: sixty percent.

The blocks make the arithmetic visible. In real substrate, that water is spread through spaces between solid parts. It does not sit in six separate water blocks.


Put litres beside the fraction

Use a larger example: a total substrate sample of five litres at sixty percent VWC. Five times zero point six is three, so this sample contains three litres of water.

Imagine labelling the sample: three litres of water already inside. That label is not a watering instruction. It does not tell you to pour another three litres into a five litre pot.


The probe sees a region

Sixty percent of the total volume is water. Is all the remaining forty percent air?

No. The remaining volume includes solid material and air. VWC alone does not give the complete air volume.

Do not read VWC like a fuel gauge showing how much usable fuel remains. Water in substrate is not all equally easy for roots to take up. Some is held more tightly. The same VWC in different substrates can mean different conditions for the plant.

Look at the probe. Its position and contact with the substrate matter. Calibration means checking how its output relates to the quantity being measured. These things affect whether the local reading is trustworthy.

A probe near one dripper might not represent a dry pot at the end of a row. Later lessons explain how to select locations that represent the zone.


Teach it back

The sensor now shows forty percent VWC. Does this reading always mean that the substrate is too dry?

No. Forty percent in one substrate can mean something different in another. Use your substrate, checked reference values, crop and current conditions to interpret it. There is no single VWC target for every system.

Do not select a target yet. Complete this sentence. Sixty percent VWC means what?

Approximately sixty percent of the measured sample volume is water. This is one correct answer.

The reading does not give the exact water volume available to the plant. One sensor does not prove that all pots have the same conditions.

Use these limits when you assess advice about VWC.

Next, a kitchen sponge will help explain saturation, water left after drainage, and the reference number in the app.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Imagine ten equal total substrate volumes. Four contain water. What is VWC? What else may occupy the remaining six?

Compare your reasoning: 40% VWC. The remainder includes solids and air; it is not automatically 60% air or 60% usable water.

A3 — The sponge, the pot and the Post-it

Use a kitchen sponge to understand saturation, water left after drainage, and why an app reference cannot change the water in a pot.


Start with a kitchen sponge

Dunk a kitchen sponge, then lift it out, dripping. Water fills the spaces inside it. Picture that as saturation.

The sponge material has not vanished. In substrate, water also fills spaces between solids. Saturation does not mean one hundred percent of the total sample is water.


Let it drip, without squeezing

Hold the sponge over the sink. Do not squeeze it. Let the excess water drip away. It still feels wet, but some spaces now contain air again.

Soaked and dripping, then wet after drainage: those are two different conditions. The same distinction matters in a pot, where roots need both water and oxygen.


From the sponge to the pot

Now wet a spare container of substrate. Let excess water drain using a suitable, repeatable check. Do not squeeze the substrate.

The water left after that check is its container capacity: water held under those particular container and drainage conditions.

Tall and shallow containers can retain different amounts. Substrate matters too. One sponge cannot tell you what every pot holds.

This drainage check describes container capacity. The app also has a field capacity reference. Check how it is set: automatic updates can follow observed wet peaks. A wet peak alone is not a drained-container measurement.

Repeat with the same substrate, container, starting condition and drainage procedure. There is no single drainage time for every setup.

Check probe placement and contact. Compare its reading with container weight and drainage. A number alone cannot show that the check worked.

Example: a one litre total substrate sample contains six hundred millilitres of water. Six hundred divided by one thousand is sixty percent VWC: volumetric water content.


A Post-it cannot add water

Imagine writing that sixty percent on a Post-it beside the sponge. Changing the Post-it to seventy percent would not add a drop of water.

The app reference works like that note. It tells the program which number to use. The edit itself adds no water.


Change the note: what happens?

You change the app reference from sixty to seventy percent. What happens to the water already in the pot?

Nothing. You changed the note, not the water. A physical change needs water to enter or leave.

And sixty percent was only our worked example. Your own container check could give a different result.

The highest spike on a graph is not automatically the drained reference. It could be a brief wet peak or a bad reading.

Think back to the sponge: dripping wet, then wet after excess water drains, then a note describing it.

The first two describe physical conditions. The note, like the app setting, describes a reference supported by a suitable check.

Next comes dryback: water content falls as water leaves. Keep the sponge and its Post-it separate mentally.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Use a kitchen sponge over a sink: soak it, lift it dripping, then let excess water drain without squeezing. Describe what changed. Write a number on a note beside it, then change the number. Did the sponge gain water? Explain how this maps to a pot and the app reference.

Compare your reasoning: You distinguish saturation from water retained after drainage, and explain that changing the reference number cannot add or remove water. A pot needs its own suitable, repeatable container check.

A4 — Dryback without the confusing maths

Use a familiar balance to understand a fall in water content, then calculate and label dryback.


Think of a bank balance

Imagine a bank balance falling from sixty dollars to forty-five. Fifteen dollars has gone. Now replace dollars with readings from the same water-content probe. Water content has fallen between two times. Growers call that fall dryback. The calculation describes the change. It cannot tell us how much went into the plant, drained away, or evaporated.


First find the size of the fall

VWC means the share of the measured sample volume occupied by water. Our example starts at sixty percent VWC and ends at forty-five percent. Subtract forty-five from sixty. The fall is fifteen percentage points. The word points matters because we are subtracting two percentage readings. Keep the starting reading and its time beside the ending reading.


Then compare the fall with the start

How large was that fall compared with the starting amount? Divide fifteen by sixty. That is one quarter. Multiply by one hundred to get twenty-five percent relative dryback. So this same fall is fifteen percentage points, or twenty-five percent of its start. Always divide by the starting reading for this calculation. A zero start cannot be used.


Your turn, with the steps supplied

Try the next example with help. Start at eighty percent VWC and finish at sixty. First subtract the end from the start. Then divide that answer by eighty and multiply by one hundred. Pause and write both answers. The fall is twenty percentage points. The relative dryback is twenty-five percent. Label each answer so another person can follow it.


Try it without the steps

Now use fifty percent to forty percent VWC. Work it out before continuing. The fall is ten percentage points. Ten divided by fifty, multiplied by one hundred, is twenty percent relative dryback. A useful record includes both readings, their times, and the labelled calculation. VWC describes water volume in the sampled region, rather than the percentage of usable water left.


Read the field before entering a number

The program uses relative dryback for its morning reference. With a sixty percent peak, a ten percent relative loss is six points. Sixty minus six gives a fifty-four percent VWC reference. Read the field help before entering a value. The reference tells the program what to compare against. The probe reading tells you what was observed.


Recall the method

Cover the examples and explain the method aloud. What are the starting and ending readings? What interval separates them? First subtract. For relative dryback, divide that fall by the start and multiply by one hundred. Finish the practice with new numbers. Before using a real record, check that its probe, units and timestamps match the situation you are describing.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Calculate both dryback descriptions for 70% to 56% VWC. Then find the VWC reference for 10% relative dryback from a 50% peak.

Compare your reasoning: 14 percentage points; 20% relative dryback; 45% VWC reference. Name the starting value used in each relative calculation.

A5 — EC: what it measures, and which EC you are looking at

Understand EC using salted water, then identify where each conductivity measurement comes from.


Start with salted water

Think of making salted water stronger by dissolving more salt in it. A conductivity meter responds to how easily electrical current passes through that water. EC is short for electrical conductivity. It is a useful clue about dissolved salts. It does not list the amount of each nutrient. Keep the material being measured and its unit beside the number.


Ask where the reading came from

Follow the water. In the supply tank, we call the solution reading feed EC. A probe in the wet substrate can measure bulk EC: the combined material around it. Pore-water EC describes the water between the particles. Runoff is collected drainage. A laboratory extract uses its own collection procedure. These names describe different measurements, even when the units match.


Worked example: label two readings

Our tank sample reads two point zero millisiemens per centimetre. That unit is written mS slash cm. Label the record feed EC. The direct conductivity output from a TEROS twelve probe reads zero point eight in the same unit. Label it bulk EC. The difference alone does not diagnose a problem. The tank solution and the wet substrate are different things.


A calculated value needs a method

Some software estimates pore-water EC from bulk measurements. An estimate uses a calculation with required inputs and a range where it is reliable. Find that documented method before interpreting the result. Changing the display name does not perform the calculation. If the measurement type is unknown, record it as unknown while you trace its source.


Your turn: finish the labels

Finish these labels aloud. The supplied solution is feed EC. A cup collected under a stated drainage procedure is what? Water in the spaces between particles is what? Pause first. The answers are runoff EC and pore-water EC. Keep the drainage collection method with the result. Use the same measurement type and method when following a trend.


Try a fresh record

Try a supply reading of one point seven and a drainage reading of two point four, both in millisiemens per centimetre. Write feed EC and runoff EC beside them. Before interpreting the difference, check collection timing, the meter check, and temperature adjustment. These two numbers cannot identify a particular excessive nutrient or describe every part of every pot.


Recall what the labels mean

Cover the labels and name the four measurement locations again. EC describes conductivity. VWC describes the share of sample volume occupied by water. Neither is a direct measurement of how much water a plant can access. Keep each reading in its crop and substrate context. A useful comparison starts with the same kind of measurement, rather than a universal number to copy.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

Label these new samples: supply solution at 1.9 mS/cm; direct TEROS 12 output at 0.6 mS/cm; water extracted by a stated laboratory procedure at 2.7 mS/cm. Explain why these numbers cannot identify an individual deficient nutrient.

Compare your reasoning: Feed EC; bulk EC; extract EC. EC gives conductivity information, not the concentration of each nutrient.

A6 — Can you trust that reading?

Check a reading as you would check a thermometer and a dated photograph: where it came from, when it arrived and whether it is reliable.


A reading is a dated local observation

A thermometer in the hallway cannot tell you the exact temperature in a bedroom. Yesterday’s photograph cannot show what the room looks like now. Treat a probe reading in the same way. Ask three separate questions. Is this the right location? Is the observation recent? Is the measurement reliable? A believable number can still fail one of those checks.


Worked example: how old is it?

The clock says two in the afternoon, or fourteen hundred. Probe A last supplied a valid observation at ten in the morning. Its reading is four hours old. Probe B updated at thirteen fifty-nine, one minute ago. We have calculated their ages. We have not established the current water content at A. A flat line might be steady conditions or missing updates.


Check identity and units

Next find the probe’s physical label and match it to the displayed room and zone. Then check units. A source value of zero point four five cubic metres per cubic metre is a fraction. Multiply by one hundred to display forty-five percent VWC. Reading it as zero point four five percent would create a hundredfold scale error.


A fresh reading can still be inaccurate

Accuracy is a separate question from age. Calibration is the method used to turn the sensor signal into a reported measurement. It must suit the sensor and substrate. Placement matters too. Air gaps near moisture-sensor needles can produce misleading readings. A probe near one dripper also samples a different place from the far side of the pot. Check the installation record.


Your turn: calculate, then check

It is fifteen thirty. The last valid observation was at fifteen twenty-five. Pause and calculate its age. The answer is five minutes. That answers freshness only. It does not prove the probe is correctly identified or calibrated. With the identity and units checked, look for suitable placement and calibration records. A recent reading still needs a trustworthy measurement behind it.


Try a changed scenario

Now compare forty-eight percent from twelve twenty with forty-three percent from sixteen nineteen. The current time is sixteen twenty. A is four hours old; B is one minute old. Check identities and last valid timestamps first. Then investigate availability and communications. Follow with appropriate physical evidence. The difference alone does not establish that every pot needs water.


Recall the three questions

Explain the three questions without looking: right probe, recent observation, reliable measurement. If sensing fails, record what you know and involve the responsible operator using the local fallback procedure. Lowering a protection to force irrigation leaves the sensing fault unresolved. Finish with a check record that separates the observation, its age, and the next step needed to trust it.


INDEPENDENT PRACTICE

At 09:45, probe A last reported 55% at 06:45. Probe B reports 50% at 09:44. Explain what is known, what remains unknown and your first three checks.

Compare your reasoning: A is three hours old; B is one minute old. Current zone uniformity is unknown. Verify identity/timestamps, availability/communications, then suitable physical and calibration evidence.

B1 — Read water and EC on the same graph

Read both axes, find day and night, and keep recorded evidence separate from a planning reference.


Start with time

Think of a car dashboard: the speedometer and fuel gauge share one panel, but tell you different things. This graph puts water content and EC on one screen. We will learn what each line means.

Read time along the bottom, like a diary laid out from left to right. Farther right means later. The shaded section is night in this example. The sensors can keep measuring through it.


Add water content

Start with the blue line: VWC, or water content by volume. Read its height against the percentage scale on the left. Use the time below to find when that reading happened.

At this example point, the reading is sixty percent VWC. The solid line joins the observations so you can follow the change over time.

A stale weather report cannot tell you the weather now. Check the age of live readings and look for missing sections. A smooth-looking line does not prove fresh, complete measurements.


Add EC and a second scale

Imagine a tiny electrical test through a sample. Dissolved salts help carry the current. EC, electrical conductivity, measures how readily current passes. Read EC on the right-hand scale, in millisiemens per centimetre.

Before interpreting EC, ask where it was measured. A number from the tank does not automatically describe the water around the roots.

Feed EC describes the irrigation solution. Bulk substrate EC measures the combined substrate region. Pore-water EC describes water in the spaces between solids. These are different measurements; identify which one your graph actually shows.

Now the two lines cross on screen. Think of the speedometer needle and fuel-gauge needle pointing the same way. Would that make speed equal fuel? Do crossing VWC and EC lines mean equal measurements?

No. One uses percent on the left; the other uses conductivity units on the right. Their screen positions depend on those scales. Follow how each measurement changes over time.

Subtracting EC from VWC would be like subtracting litres from kilometres per hour. The quantities and units do not match. EC alone is not a complete nutrient recipe either.


Events, observations and references

On this teaching chart, delivery markers show when watering started. In the app, a dot marks a shot start; a ring marks a shot the watering software held. Neither alone measures water delivered. VWC may rise after water reaches the probe, then fall between deliveries.

EC does not have to mirror VWC. Delivery, mixing, drainage, temperature, measurement method and crop conditions can affect it. Seeing two changes together does not prove that one caused the other.

Think of a journey plan beside the trip you actually drove. The dashed planning reference comes from software settings. The measured line shows observations. They need different labels even if they happen to follow the same path.


Continue into night; move a threshold

Follow the planning line through the shaded night to the next morning. Like a route drawn on a map, it should be readable across the whole journey. A continuous line is still only a reference, not proof of tomorrow's crop response.

Here the EC reference joins the last daytime reference to the next morning reference. The overnight phase is called P3. This join is not a separately scheduled P3 EC target, and it does not calculate how salts will behave overnight.

Now watch this demo edit. We increase the P3 emergency VWC threshold in the local draft. The draft threshold changes from thirty-five percent to thirty-nine percent. The saved threshold remains visible for comparison. The measured example readings remain the same.


Explain what changed

Which item changed? The previous water content? The local draft of a threshold? Or a guaranteed irrigation event?

Only the local draft changed. Think of a fridge alarm limit: it marks an exceptional condition, not the normal temperature you aim for. This emergency water threshold is not a normal overnight target. Review and apply are separate actions; the watering software, called the Controller, still checks its conditions and protection rules before irrigation.

Point to the time axis. Point to the VWC scale. Point to the EC scale. Identify one irrigation marker. Identify one planning reference. Find the reading age.

B2 — P0 to P3: build the day one phase at a time

Follow a watering day as four familiar jobs: wait, refill, maintain and allow dryback; then attach the program’s P0–P3 labels.


Four jobs across the watering day

Think of the watering day as a journey with four jobs. Wait while the root zone dries in the morning. Refill with watering events. Maintain water content during the active period. Then allow dryback overnight. The program labels these jobs: P zero, P one, P two and P three. A phase simply means one part of that sequence.


Wait first: morning dryback, P0

The first job is waiting. At configured lights-on, a new grow-day begins in P zero, called morning dryback. Water content can continue falling before refill begins. The controller checks the dryback rule and timing limits, including a maximum wait. Lights-on alone therefore does not supply an exact first-shot time. Match the configured lighting schedule with the room.


Refill: ramp-up, P1

The second job is refill. The program calls it P one, or ramp-up. A shot means one watering event. Several shots can raise water content toward the configured target, with checks between events. The target is a comparison value used by the rule. The probe response also depends on delivery and drainage. Count completed events from records rather than from a drawing.


Maintain: conditional watering, P2

The third job is maintenance, called P two. A re-water threshold is the reference used when deciding whether another shot is needed. Readings, timing rules and protections still apply. A planning chart may show evenly spaced shots using an assumed drying rate. If actual drying is slower, fewer events may occur. Read the controller’s reason before calling a missing shot a fault.


Worked example: a 06:00–18:00 lighting day

Use a fictional room lit from six in the morning to six in the evening. At six, begin the morning wait. When its rules allow, move to refill, then maintenance. Daytime watering can finish before lights-off. The overnight period follows. Explain those four jobs in order. We cannot calculate the refill and maintenance transition times from lighting hours alone.


Overnight dryback: P3

The fourth job is overnight dryback, called P three. The emergency floor is a separate low-water reference for the overnight rescue rule. It is not a nightly destination to aim for. A rescue event depends on its conditions and protections; the label overnight does not order an automatic night shot. Unreliable sensing needs the local fallback procedure and the responsible operator.


Your turn: explain another day

Pause and fill the gaps. Morning wait is P zero. Maintenance is P two. Now explain a room lit from eight in the morning to eight in the evening using all four jobs. Which shot times remain unknown? Recall that a forecast describes assumed events, while the event record describes completed actions. Moving a reference line cannot change yesterday’s observed readings.

B3 — Vegetative and generative: a bias, not a magic switch

Use a dimmer-switch analogy to separate a steering direction from the planner’s numerical blend and the crop’s actual response.


Think of a dimmer

A dimmer moves gradually between two ends of a range. Keep that picture for the planner slider. First name the directions. Vegetative growth includes roots, leaves and shoots. Generative growth includes reproductive structures, such as flowers and fruit. A steering bias is an intended influence on that balance. It is separate from the plant’s stage and does not guarantee a response.


The plant responds to its conditions

Growers influence root-zone conditions through irrigation timing, dryback and suitable EC bounds. Climate and light also matter. In suitable conditions, smaller drybacks can support a vegetative direction; greater dryback can contribute to a generative direction. These are broad tendencies. Substrate, plant variety and stage change the response. A flowering plant may still need a vegetative bias to support balanced growth.


What the slider actually calculates

An endpoint is a set of values at one end of the slider. A profile stores both sets for reuse. Zero selects the configured vegetative endpoint. One hundred selects the generative endpoint. Between them, the planner blends supported settings. The grower supplies the bounds. Pot size and dripper flow help describe the setup, but cannot choose the biological targets automatically.


Worked example: halfway

Take fictional morning-wait endpoints of thirty and fifty minutes. Their difference is twenty. At slider fifty, use half the difference: ten minutes. Add ten to thirty. The result is forty minutes, before rounding to the setting’s supported step. This calculation explains the slider. Choosing suitable endpoints needs knowledge of the actual crop and substrate.


Your turn, with the steps supplied

Keep thirty and fifty as the endpoints. Set the slider to twenty-five. What fraction of the difference is that? Pause and calculate. One quarter of twenty is five. B4 — How much water did each plant get?

Treat drippers as small taps, calculate delivered volume from flow and runtime, then separate delivery from drainage and plant uptake.


A dripper is a small tap

Picture each dripper as a small tap. Its flow rate says how much water it delivers in a given time. Four litres per hour, written four L slash h, means four litres in one hour at that stated flow. Two such emitters supply each example plant. A rated flow is a label; a timed catch test checks actual delivery.


Worked example: thirty seconds

Our completed watering event lasts thirty seconds. Two emitters at four litres per hour give eight litres per hour per plant. There are three thousand six hundred seconds in an hour. Multiply eight by thirty, then divide by three thousand six hundred. That is about zero point zero six six seven litres, or sixty-six point seven millilitres per plant.


Scale from one plant to twelve

The zone has twelve plants. Multiply the unrounded per-plant amount by twelve. The result is zero point eight litres for the whole zone. Per plant and per zone describe different scopes. For a longer shot at the same flow, the estimated volume rises in proportion to runtime. For a daily total, use completed events rather than every projected event on a plan.


A volume fraction is not a VWC response

Compare a separate forty-five-second shot using those same two emitters. It delivers zero point one litres, or one hundred millilitres per plant. In five litres of substrate, that volume is two percent of substrate volume. It does not promise a two-point VWC rise. Water can spread unevenly or drain, and the probe samples only part of the container.


Your turn: complete the calculation

Try one emitter at three litres per hour for sixty seconds. Multiply three by sixty and divide by three thousand six hundred. Pause and finish it. The answer is zero point zero five litres, or fifty millilitres per plant. Multiply by twelve plants to get zero point six litres per zone. State whether the flow came from a rating or a catch test.


Use the time that actually ran

A request can be shortened by the controller’s duration limit. If one hundred twenty seconds was requested but sixty was effective, use sixty in the estimate. Runtime still cannot prove equal flow from every outlet. Pressure and blocked emitters can change delivery. Commissioning checks actual catches, correct outlets and that water stops, with the responsible person supervising the test.


Recall: delivery, drainage, uptake

Finish with two emitters at three litres per hour for ninety seconds and fifteen plants. Calculate before continuing: zero point one five litres per plant and two point two five litres per zone. Delivery means supplied water. Runoff means water leaving by drainage. Uptake means water entering the plant. A flow-and-time delivery estimate measures neither runoff nor uptake.

C1 — What you need: the smallest useful system

Plan equipment by function: host, measurement, water path, control and independent physical protection.


Five functions, not a shopping spree

Start with one zone: plants whose water delivery is controlled together. Prove that small system works before expanding.

Think of watering by hand: you need somewhere to make the plan, a way to check wetness, a water supply, a way to start and stop it, and checks against mistakes. An automated system needs those same five functions.


Host and measurement paths

Every app needs a computer to run on. Here that computer is called the host. Choose one supported route: a Raspberry Pi, or a dedicated Intel NUC or other x86-64 computer. You only need one.

Like any computer, the host needs the correct power supply, suitable storage for its software and data, and a reliable network connection.

The two host lessons are alternative routes to the same starting point. Follow the one for your chosen computer.

Think of labels on a switchboard: one label identifies a reading or a control. Home Assistant calls these named items entities. Crop Steering uses those entities. You need both the Crop Steering integration and the separate Controller app; a dashboard display alone cannot run irrigation. The integration holds room settings and the workspace. The Controller reads those settings and decides when to water.

The moisture probe is the system's local wetness check. Choose a model suitable for the substrate and a position that represents the zone. Verify its exact model, connection interface and units, rather than buying on appearance.

Some probes need a bridge: equipment that translates their connection into one the system can use. Matching wire colours do not prove compatibility. One working probe cannot prove equal delivery to every pot.

EC needs a location label too. Bulk substrate EC describes a measurement through the substrate region; pore-water EC describes the water between solids. Feed EC describes the solution supplied to the crop. Check what the sensor actually measures.

A tank reading stays a tank reading when you rename its label. Calling feed EC root-zone EC cannot move the measurement into the pot.

Air temperature and humidity help explain changing water demand, just as laundry dries differently on a hot, dry day than a cool, damp one.


Draw the actual water path

Trace water from its source, as you would trace a garden hose. A suitable pressurised supply may feed a zone valve directly. A reservoir needs a pump if it cannot provide the required pressure.

Do not add a pump just because a diagram has one. If your water path requires a pump, include that pump and the protection suited to it.

Follow the whole path: filtration, pressure regulation, drainage, and protection against reverse flow or unwanted siphoning. These must suit the actual equipment and meet applicable local requirements.


Safe control and proof

Starting a pump can demand more power than keeping it running, like the extra push needed to get a loaded trolley moving. Control hardware needs suitable starting and operating ratings and correct installation. A household smart plug's large printed rating alone does not prove suitability.

Use an appropriately qualified installer for electrical work. Do not do mains electrical work as part of this course.

Two people turning the same tap can defeat each other's instructions. Give each physical pump or valve one controller. An old irrigation system must not also operate that output.

Find and resolve competing control before enabling the new Controller.

The final function is protection and proof. Provide an accessible physical shutdown, suitable independent protection and somewhere safe to collect test water. Software OFF is like a stop request on a screen; it is not an emergency isolator.

Use collection cups and a stopwatch to measure what actually arrives. Then observe the outlet after the stop command: the water must physically stop.

A changed dashboard icon is a report from software. Look at the water outlet to prove stopping.


Essentials, conditional, later

Build the basic working zone before adding instruments. Tank level, feed EC, pH, which describes acidity, and tank temperature add operating information. More probes and a verified flow meter can follow. Automated dosing, thermal cameras and artificial intelligence are not needed for the first working zone.


Your own layout

Sketch your water route like a simple road map. Label the supply, pump if installed, zone valve, drippers and drainage path.

Beside that map, draw the probe and the Controller. Mark the physical shutdown and identify the one controller responsible for each output.

Circle each item that you cannot identify.

Check exact models using the equipment sheet before buying. Choose the host lesson for your Raspberry Pi or x86-64 computer.

Both host routes then join the same setup procedure: install one zone, assign its sensor readings and water controls, and verify physical operation before adding more zones.

Moving from Growlink is like moving house: label what you have before disconnecting anything. Begin with observation and available records. Name one room, one physical zone, its probes, valves, pump and current controller. Record who can operate or recover it. A working Growlink setup is useful evidence about the existing system, not proof that the same hardware can connect to Home Assistant. Keep the existing operator responsible for crop care while planning.


Keep the records in their original context

Use exports available in your actual Growlink account and product, or record visible settings and history manually. Do not assume an export feature, API or automatic importer exists. Preserve original files and dates. Note the timezone, lights-on and lights-off, substrate, plant and dripper counts, measured flow, targets and measurement methods. A number labelled EC needs its location and method. Similar field names in two systems do not guarantee the same meaning.


Example: one old zone, several new addresses

Imagine Growlink calls a valve Room A, Zone One. Your Home Assistant setup now has an entity with the same friendly name. That is a contact-book match, not physical proof. Trace the actual output and probe, confirm compatible interfaces, units and data age, and write exact new IDs beside the old record. Keep unknown items marked unknown. Reuse C4’s sizing and mapping checks; do not paste old targets into differently defined fields.


Agree the handover and the way back

With the responsible operator and competent installer, agree when ownership changes, which controls are disabled, and how the crop receives necessary water during work. Establish one controller per physical output. The new system may observe where supported; it must not command the same valve alongside Growlink. Record physical isolation and the steps to restore the previous arrangement if checks fail. A rollback plan is a checked procedure, not permission to leave both schedules active.


Your turn: the name matches, the unit does not

Review a different migration record. The old sensor is labelled EC, its method is unknown, and the new field expects pore-water EC. The valve name matches, but no physical identity check exists. Decide what to do. Park those mappings and investigate; a familiar display cannot close either gap. Keep the last verified arrangement under its agreed owner. Finish the checks from C3 to C5 before planning C6’s supervised catch and Controller stages.


Prove one zone before expanding

At the agreed handover, follow the approved shutdown and ownership procedure, then complete staged commissioning from C6. Check acknowledged mapping, fresh readings, real flow, stopping, approved targets and timing. Supervise the first enabled cycle and record its event and final state. If a check fails, leave the new output disabled and use the agreed recovery procedure. Expand only after this zone’s evidence is complete. Repeat for every output; one successful zone does not establish the rest.

D1 — Find your room, zone and next action

Use the room and zone like a full address, then read recent evidence and the controller’s reason.


A zone needs its full address

A shopping-centre directory helps you find a shop, but you still need its full address. Use the dashboard the same way. Open Overview in the isolated demo. All zones groups cards by room. Select the room, then open a zone from Selected room. Zone two in Room A is a different address from Zone two in Room B.


Check when each source last reported

Before interpreting a number, check its age. The header reports the controller’s latest communication. The probe has its own observation time and unit. A recent controller message cannot refresh an old probe reading. Unknown means the program cannot confirm a state. Keep that word in your record. Replacing an unknown pump state with OFF would invent certainty.


Read the reason beside the state

Open the zone page and find its phase, latest irrigation and Why / next action. Read them together. A waiting phase may be doing exactly what its rules require. The reason tells you what condition matters next. Inspect tank and equipment information where available. A recorded event describes an action; an output state describes a report. Physical delivery needs its own evidence.


Worked example: Training East, Zone 2

In this fictional paper example, training East is selected. Zone two is in maintenance, P two, and its last shot was at nine twelve. Recent reports say it is waiting for moisture. That is the supported explanation for the pause. Header Watering and Stop all address the selected room, Training East. Read the shared-pump caution: stopping a shared pump can also affect another room.


Your turn: finish an honest record

Now select Room B, Zone one. Record the pump state and report age exactly as shown. If a source says unknown, record that uncertainty. Next inspect the mapped source and its report age under the local procedure. Mapping means the connection between the displayed record and the real device. Pressing buttons is not a way to discover that connection.

D2 — Change one target and see the curve move

Treat a target change like editing and checking a shopping list: draft, review, apply, read back and restore.


Begin with a pencil draft

A shopping list written in pencil can change before checkout. A draft target is similar: it is a proposed local edit. Open Irrigation, Current day, in the isolated demo and choose a zone. Read Target source first. A plan can own these same targets, making them read-only here. Choose a manual demo zone for practice, leaving real plan ownership alone.


Tell the three lines apart

A reference is a value used for comparison. The saved reference shows the available configuration. The draft reference shows your proposed change. The actual trace shows recorded observations from the probe. Read those names in the legend. Moving the draft line changes the proposal on screen. It cannot move water already in the pot or rewrite yesterday’s readings.


Learn what the field means

For this exercise, open the help beside P three emergency floor. Think of it as a night alarm: a low-water safety reference used by overnight rules. It is not the water content to maintain routinely all night. The rule still has conditions and protections. Read the unit and allowed step, then record the original demo value before editing.


Worked example: 30 becomes a 31 draft

Record the original demo value and allowed step. For a paper example, thirty with one-point steps becomes thirty-one. Watch its reference move while the saved reference remains. Review changes is like checking the basket before checkout. Read the room, zone, field, unit, old value and new value. Apply is the action that sends the reviewed request to Home Assistant; the isolated demo changes sample state.


Check the receipt, then restore

After sending a request, check its receipt. Here, readback means inspecting the value returned by the system, along with any error. Sending a change does not prove acceptance. If the result is unclear, resolve that uncertainty before repeating it. Restore the original value you recorded through the same draft, review, apply and readback sequence. Practising the return path completes the exercise.


Try another manual demo zone

Choose another manual demo zone. Use its own original value and allowed step, rather than copying thirty and thirty-one. Predict which line will move. Make one step, review it, apply, check readback, then restore and check again. If a field is room-wide, inspect its scope carefully. A zone-specific value can override a shared room value for that zone.


Recall the complete change journey

Explain the shopping-list journey without looking at the screen. Draft proposes. Review checks the complete change. Apply sends it. Readback checks what was accepted. Tell another learner which line records observations and which shows a reference. Plan-owned dated targets are managed through Calendar. Finish by checking that the demo value is restored and noting both returned values in your practice record.

D3 — Plan days and weeks without losing control

Build an editable demo timetable from named bundles of settings, then separate storing, arming, activation and Watering permission.


A timetable points to a settings card

Think of a calendar timetable that points to a recipe card. The timetable says when; the card holds a bundle of settings. The planner calls that bundle a profile. Open Irrigation, Calendar, using an already editable demo draft. A draft is work still being prepared. Read its plan status and target ownership before opening any settings.


A profile holds two endpoints

Open Calendar and profiles, then Endpoint profiles. Each profile holds two sets of values, called endpoints: vegetative and generative. The balance slider blends between them, as you practised in B three. One profile can serve several schedule blocks and zones. Find those uses before editing. Duplicate the profile when you need an independent copy for another zone.


Worked example: a 2%–4% blend

Use fictional P two shot-size endpoints of two and four percent of substrate volume. Halfway gives three percent, before rounding to the supported step. This is a delivery-size setting, not a promise of a three-point probe rise. Give the demo zone its own start date. Assign the profile to days one through seven, checking neighbouring blocks for missing days or overlaps.


Store first, then request activation

Review and save stores the checked draft in Home Assistant. Arm is a separate request for the controller to start using it at the next local lights-on. Activation means the controller has taken over its dated targets. Check validation, controller support and reported status. Arming neither turns Watering on nor starts the pump. Those are separate control permissions and actions.


Understand the return handover

Disarm asks for a handover back to manual targets at the next lights-on. Existing active targets remain until that handover. Review the manual values that will return and wait for draft status before editing. A plan hold pauses plan steering, while controller safety watering may still occur. For uncontrolled physical flow, the local physical shutdown procedure takes priority.


Your turn: another dated block

Use another demo zone and days eight through fourteen. Say its start date, find every block sharing the chosen profile, and preview the dated values. Review the draft without arming real equipment. Recipes stores reusable copies for this browser, site, room and mode. Loading a copy creates local draft work; saving a library copy does not change active Home Assistant targets.


Recall what each action does

Close the screen and teach the timetable analogy. What does a profile contain? When does day one start for each zone? Explain save, arm, activation and Watering separately. Then explain when disarm returns manual control. Tomorrow repeat with a different zone start date. Your practice record should show the edited block and its preview, with shared uses and unresolved status clearly identified.

D4 — Compare reality with the plan

Read recorded history like a bank statement and a target reference like a budget, comparing equivalent periods and preserving source labels.


A budget and a statement answer different questions

A bank budget describes a plan. A bank statement records transactions. Keep that distinction when reading History in the isolated demo. Timeline lists events and changes. Water use shows delivery records. Compare runs helps compare available histories; Grows manages run records and summaries. Start by writing the question, room, zone and interval you want the evidence to answer.


Match the comparison before calculating

Compare the same elapsed grow age, such as day ten with day ten. Match the zone definition and check whether plant count changed. Then check units and measurement types, including which EC is displayed. Inspect the recorded dates and coverage. A full week and one day offer different opportunities for water use. Missing periods stay missing, rather than becoming zeros.


Worked example: day ten, 12 L versus 15 L

Two fictional runs have complete day-ten records for the same zone definition. Run A reports twelve litres estimated delivery. Run B reports fifteen. Subtract twelve from fifteen: B reports three litres more estimated delivery for that matched day. Preserve the word estimated. These totals cannot tell us that plants absorbed three litres more or that a changed target caused the difference.


Date the reference as carefully as the reading

A reference is a comparison value or configuration, like the budget beside the statement. A saved run can preserve a timestamped snapshot of its reference. Creating last month’s run record today captures today’s available reference, unless an existing saved reference is available. It cannot reconstruct last month’s targets or deleted readings. Read the reference source and capture date beside the actual trace.


Your turn: name what the estimate means

Complete the sentence: runtime multiplied by configured flow estimates delivery, rather than plant what? Pause. The answer is uptake: water entering the plant. A per-plant figure also averages the zone total over its plant count. It does not prove every emitter supplied equally. Keep estimated, measured or unknown beside the source when you copy values into a report.


Try an incomplete comparison

Now one record covers day six only; another covers days one through six. An EC series is missing. Decide whether their totals are directly comparable before calculating. They are not. Choose a matched interval with adequate coverage. Write one supported observation and one unanswered question. A longer chart axis cannot recover data the recorder no longer retains.


Recall an observation before proposing a cause

Without the chart, explain the difference between an actual observation, a saved reference and a current reference. Repeat the day-ten delivery comparison in one sentence. Then name other possible influences: climate, plant count, flow assumptions and other changes. Finish your practice with scope, interval, units, source and coverage limits. Those details let the next person assess the comparison and choose the next useful check.

Compare your reasoning: The observation names scope, interval, units and source; the limit avoids inventing past targets, uptake or causation.

D5 — Troubleshoot before you change a recipe

Separate uncontrolled physical flow from bad information, restore trustworthy evidence and verify the actual repair.


Repair the evidence before turning the dial

Imagine a fridge thermometer with a dead battery. Turning the thermostat does not repair the missing measurement. Apply that idea to irrigation faults. First check for uncontrolled water or unsafe equipment. If water continues unexpectedly, use the installation’s physical shutdown procedure immediately. Once safe, preserve what happened. For a faulty reading without uncontrolled flow, investigate the source through the local fallback procedure.


Worked example: a four-hour-old reading

A fictional zone is holding and its moisture observation is four hours old. Lowering a threshold would leave the old information unresolved. Record the room, zone, timestamp and hold reason. Check the mapped source, communication path and probe condition under the local procedure. Use appropriate independent evidence with competent help. Verify fresh observations and recovered status before reconsidering targets.


Conflicting tank evidence needs a real check

The tank display says eighty percent, but the installed low-level indication says low. These conflict. Averaging them invents a level; choosing eighty because it is reassuring leaves the fault unresolved. Keep protections in use. Check physical level safely, then source identity, calibration and timestamps. Starting the pump is not a test for an empty tank. Record which evidence resolves the disagreement.


A stored setup needs controller acceptance

Saving setup confirms that configuration was stored. Acknowledgement means the controller reports which setup revision it accepted. Check that acceptance, its current heartbeat and setup problems; a heartbeat is a recent communication report. Keep affected watering disabled until acceptance is resolved under the local procedure. Competent commissioning must still prove device identity, the intended outlet’s operation and actual stopping.


Your turn: OFF displayed, water flowing

The screen says OFF but water is visibly flowing. Finish the first response: physical shutdown. After the installation is safe, competent help can investigate a sticking valve, siphon, incorrect mapping or competing control owners. Header Stop all is a selected-room software action. A returned OFF state cannot prove stopped flow. Disarm and plan hold also cannot replace the physical procedure.


Try another fault report independently

A selected zone shows no water-content rise after an event, while its neighbour rises. Write the observation before naming a cause. Check zone identities, timestamps and commissioning records; consider placement and delivery. Arrange supported equipment checks with competent help rather than forcing valves in turn. Report room, zone, event time, expected result and actual result. Exclude credentials from the report.


Recall the repair sequence

Teach the sequence without looking: make safe, preserve evidence, investigate, verify. Explain how a sensor repair differs from a target adjustment. Verification includes recovered readings and the agreed physical final state, with supervised recommissioning where needed. State any local shutdown knowledge you still need from the responsible operator. Finish with the fault record, the repair evidence and the person responsible for the next check.

D6 — A five-minute daily check and a useful handover

Use a short preflight and a clear diary to make one defensible decision, then leave a handover another operator can use.


A short preflight, then a brief diary

A pilot checks the aircraft before departure, then records the flight. Use the same rhythm for the daily check: inspect first, leave a brief diary afterwards. The record has five parts: identity and freshness; state; water; feed and context; decision. Five minutes is a useful routine, not a deadline for an unresolved fault. Record unavailable sources explicitly.


Fill the first four parts with evidence

Start with room, zone, time and ages of the controller and relevant probes. State means the phase, Why / next action, last irrigation and confirmed output reports. Water means the delivery total and any per-plant average, labelled measured, estimated or unknown. Feed and context means tank indications, the EC measurement type and relevant crop or climate notes. These give the final decision a basis.


Worked example: a reasoned no change

In a fictional paper example, training North, Zone three, has recent reports, the expected maintenance phase and a clear waiting reason. Estimated delivery is within its recent observed range. Tank indications agree. The decision can be no change. Write that reason and when you will check again. The value of the check is a defensible decision. Moving a slider is not a required outcome.


Your turn: finish the change record

For a justified change, record scope, before and after, reason and the observation you expect next. A draft is the proposed edit. Review checks it, apply sends it, and readback checks the returned accepted value. Complete the sequence with a follow-up time. If several things change, record them all. One better day cannot isolate which change caused the difference.


Use Crop AI as an assistant

Crop AI can organise a question or explain a proposal. It does not measure the crop, and confident wording does not verify a claim. Check the readings, timestamps, room, zone, units and assumptions behind its suggestion. Review and verify any authorised action through the normal workflow. Keep credentials out of prompts and screenshots. Advanced AI configuration is unnecessary for this daily routine.


Try another day and prepare the handover

Choose another demo zone and record what is available. For a paper variation, suppose one source is unavailable. Complete the five parts and decide whether to retain settings, investigate or make one justified demo adjustment. Explain why. A handover names the responsible person, software versions, backup location, recovery procedure and agreed final state. A listed backup alone cannot prove it restores successfully. Preserve a recoverable backup and follow the local update and rollback guide.


Teach it back, then recall it later

Teach another learner to find the full room-and-zone address, check freshness and explain control scope. Demonstrate the change workflow in the demo. Physical shutdown and commissioning evidence need competent local handover. Tomorrow recall draft, apply and arm without notes. In about a week, solve a stale-data case with changed values. Keep your diary brief, specific and clear about unresolved checks.
